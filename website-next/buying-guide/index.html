Buying guide

What to have before an emergency, and what to look for when you buy it.

First aid

Treat small injuries yourself when help is slow to come.

A stocked first aid kit

Cuts, burns and sprains still happen when clinics are closed.

What to look for

  • Sterile gauze pads in 2-inch and 4-inch sizes, roller bandages, triangular bandages and adhesive tape. FEMA
  • Non-latex exam gloves, scissors, tweezers and a thermometer. FEMA
  • Antibiotic ointment, a hot and cold compress, and bandages in assorted sizes. FEMA

A first aid manual on paper

Paper works with no power at all.

What to look for

  • FEMA lists a first aid kit “and manual”. Keep the two together. FEMA

Spare medicines

Pharmacies may be closed or out of stock for days.

What to look for

  • A supply of each prescription, plus pain relievers, anti-diarrhea medicine, antacids and laxatives. Ready.gov (FEMA)
  • Replace anything past its date. Check the whole kit once a year. Ready.gov (FEMA)

Drinking water

Water comes first. Store some, and have a way to make more safe.

Stored water

One gallon per person per day, for drinking, food and washing. Two weeks if you can.

What to look for

  • Commercially bottled water is the safest. Keep it sealed and watch the use-by date. FEMA
  • For your own water, use food-grade storage containers. Not milk or juice jugs, and not glass. FEMA

A water filter

Removes dirt, parasites and some bacteria from water you collect.

What to look for

  • Absolute pore size of 1 micron or smaller, rated to NSF Standard 53 or 58 for cyst reduction. National Park Service
  • Filters can’t remove viruses. Boil or disinfect the water too. National Park Service

Plain unscented bleach and a dropper

A few drops make clear water safe when you can’t boil it.

What to look for

  • Regular, unscented chlorine bleach. The label should say 6% or 8.25% sodium hypochlorite. EPA
  • Not scented, color-safe, or with added cleaners. Less than a year old. EPA
  • 8 drops of 6% bleach, or 6 drops of 8.25%, per gallon. Wait 30 minutes. EPA

Food preparation and preservation

Eat what you stored, cook without the grid, and keep food safe.

Food that keeps, and a manual can opener

At least several days of food that needs no fridge.

What to look for

  • Non-perishable food your household already eats. Include a manual can opener and utensils. Ready.gov (FEMA)
  • Ready-to-eat canned food and high-energy foods like peanut butter and crackers. FEMA

A fridge and freezer thermometer

Tells you what to keep and what to throw out after the power goes off.

What to look for

  • Throw out perishable food that sat above 40°F for two hours or more. Ready.gov (FEMA)

A camp stove and fuel

Cook and boil water when the stove at home doesn’t work.

What to look for

  • A small cooking stove with a can of its fuel. FEMA
  • Outdoors only. Never use a camp stove, grill or generator indoors. Ready.gov (FEMA)

A pressure canner

Preserves a harvest for months without a freezer.

What to look for

  • Low-acid foods must be processed in a pressure canner. National Center for Home Food Preservation (USDA)
  • Underwriters Laboratories (UL) approval. National Center for Home Food Preservation (USDA)
  • A dial gauge needs checking for accuracy every year. A weighted gauge doesn’t. National Center for Home Food Preservation (USDA)

Critical repairs

Stop the damage from getting worse until help arrives.

A wrench to turn off utilities

A gas leak or a burst pipe can’t wait for a plumber.

What to look for

  • A wrench or pliers that fit your gas and water shutoffs. Learn how before you need it. FEMA
  • For gas, a non-sparking wrench. Only a licensed technician should turn the gas back on. FEMA

Duct tape, plastic sheeting and a tarp

Seal a broken window, cover a damaged roof, or shelter in place.

What to look for

  • Plastic sheeting, scissors and duct tape. Ready.gov (FEMA)
  • A tarp for a damaged roof, to stop more damage. FEMA

A fire extinguisher

Fire crews may be far away or busy.

What to look for

  • At least an A-B-C type, for most home fires. FEMA
  • A “UL Listed” label. The largest size you can use safely and comfortably. U.S. Fire Administration (FEMA)

Power

Light, charge and stay safe when the grid is down.

Flashlights and extra batteries

Use them instead of candles.

What to look for

  • One flashlight for every person in the house. Ready.gov (FEMA)
  • Extra batteries in the sizes they take. Ready.gov (FEMA)

A backup battery for your phone

Keeps a phone charged for calls, texts and alerts.

What to look for

  • Chargers and a backup battery, kept charged. Ready.gov (FEMA)
  • A solar charger can top it up during a long outage. FEMA

Carbon monoxide alarms

Generators, stoves and heaters make a gas you can’t see or smell.

What to look for

  • Battery backup. One in a central place on every level of the home. Ready.gov (FEMA)

A generator, used safely

Runs a fridge, a pump or medical equipment through a long outage.

What to look for

  • Outdoors only, at least 20 feet from windows, doors and attached garages. Ready.gov (FEMA)
  • Plug appliances in with heavy-duty extension cords. Ready.gov (FEMA)
  • Never connect it to the house wiring unless a licensed electrician installs an approved transfer switch. Generac

General emergency preparedness

The basics for any emergency, kept together and ready to grab.

A weather radio

Hear warnings and news when phones and internet are down.

What to look for

  • Battery-powered or hand-crank, and a NOAA Weather Radio with tone alert. Ready.gov (FEMA)
  • NOAA Weather Radio needs a radio made to receive it. National Weather Service
  • A “Public Alert” feature. FEMA

A whistle and dust masks

Signal for help, and filter dusty or smoky air.

What to look for

  • A whistle to signal for help, and a dust mask to help filter contaminated air. Ready.gov (FEMA)

Containers that carry it all

If you have to leave fast, the kit goes with you.

What to look for

  • Items in airtight plastic bags, in one or two easy-to-carry bins or a duffel bag. Ready.gov (FEMA)
  • A waterproof container for copies of documents, and one for matches. Ready.gov (FEMA)

Warm bedding

Heat may be off for days.

What to look for

  • A sleeping bag or warm blanket for each person. Ready.gov (FEMA)
  • A change of clothes for your climate and sturdy shoes. Ready.gov (FEMA)
